**Use this weekend to test whether one recent graduate will pay for a small resume edit.** Treat this as a demand test before committing to the business.

Your biggest uncertainty is willingness to pay. A concrete offer gives you more useful evidence than asking, “Would you use this?”

Spend your five hours this way:

1. **Define a narrow offer — 30 minutes.** For example: “I’ll edit your one-page resume for clarity and relevance to one job posting, with tracked changes and one revision.” Choose a single test price you’d be willing to work for. That price is an experiment, not an established market rate. Promise only work you can confidently deliver; avoid promises about interviews or jobs.

2. **Reach potential buyers — 90 minutes.** Send personal messages to recent graduates you can reach, or post in a relevant community that permits offers. Aim for 10 relevant contacts as a manageable target. Ask about their current job search, then make the offer where it fits.

   “Are you applying for jobs right now? I’m testing a resume-editing service for recent graduates: one-page resume, tailored to one posting, with tracked edits and one revision for [price]. Would you like to book a slot this weekend?”

3. **Follow up and deliver — 2 hours.** Answer questions and, if someone pays, complete the agreed work. If nobody buys, use the time to understand objections without pressuring people.

4. **Review the evidence — 1 hour.** Record contacts, replies, offers made, payments, objections, and delivery time in a simple note.

**Your decision rule:** One payment justifies another small test; it doesn’t prove a business. Interested replies without payment leave willingness to pay unresolved. Few replies may mean you haven’t reached people effectively.

Your first action is to write the offer and list five recent graduates you could contact. No folders, operating system, or project setup needed.
